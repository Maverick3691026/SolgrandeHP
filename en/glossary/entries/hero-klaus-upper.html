The Tale of Hero Klaus, Volume One: Transformation

A fairy tale handed down across Brenoria, depicting Klaus’s childhood and the beginning of his path as a soldier.

Work Information

Title
The Tale of Hero Klaus
Volume
Volume One: Transformation
Classification
Fairy tale
Region
Brenoria continent

Story

In northern Ernest lay a lake whose waters appeared deep crimson throughout the year.

Lake Lacast.

Layers rich in reddish minerals covered the lake bed,

while minute algae bearing red pigment lived in its waters.

Light passing through the clear water reflected from the minerals below and mingled with the algae’s color,

making the lake appear deep crimson from afar.

In later ages it would be counted among Ernest’s great sights as the “Crimson Lake.” In those days,

however, it was simply part of everyday life for the people nearby—

a place to fish,

draw water, and read the changing seasons.

On its shore stood a small village called Engosa.

It was a quiet place surrounded by forest and lake, far from the great roads.

Aside from the occasional peddler or traveler, it had little contact with the outside world.

Most villagers tilled the fields and lived on the forest’s bounty.

Klaus was born in Engosa.

His father was quiet but kind, and his mother gentle and wise.

Their home was neither large nor prosperous, yet young Klaus rarely thought of himself as poor.

Each morning his mother lit the hearth, and he woke to its sound.

His father prepared for work, stroked the still-drowsy boy’s head once with a broad hand, left a few words behind, and departed.

Soon the smell of breakfast filled the house.

Outside came the cries of chickens and the footsteps of villagers setting out to work.

Such mornings were Klaus’s ordinary life.

His father was not a man of many words.

Even after returning from work, he seldom spoke of his day and said only what was needed at the table.

Yet whenever Klaus began a story, however long it became, his father never interrupted.

He listened silently to the end.

A strangely shaped nut found in the forest.

A small animal chased with the village children.

A bird at Lake Lacast unlike any he had seen before.

They were momentous to a child and perhaps trivial to an adult.

Even so, his father smiled, listened, and occasionally answered with a brief nod.

From that alone, Klaus knew his words had reached him.

In winter, his father’s presence felt greater still.

Winters in Engosa were severe, and winds from Lake Lacast shook the walls each night.

Whenever the fire weakened, his father added wood and kept it alive until the family slept.

When drowsy Klaus leaned against him, he stroked the boy’s back without a word.

The hand was hard and knotted, scarred by labor in forest and field.

Yet while that hand touched him, Klaus feared nothing, however fiercely the wind howled outside.

His mother, meanwhile, supported every corner of the family’s life.

She timed meals for his father’s early departures, washed Klaus’s mud-soaked clothes with an exasperated look, cleaned every scrape, and woke through the night to feel his forehead when fever came.

She knew his father’s favorite dishes and the foods Klaus disliked.

As winter approached, she checked every garment, mended each frayed seam, and gathered what was missing before the snow arrived.

For her, nothing mattered more than seeing the family finish another day safely, sleep beneath one roof, and greet the next morning together.

Klaus grew within that small repetition.

On mild days the three sometimes walked to Lake Lacast.

The road was a perfect playground for Klaus.

He ran ahead, discovered something, and hurried back to his parents each time.

They never rushed him and stayed until his curiosity was spent.

By the time they reached the shore, the sun had begun to set.

The naturally crimson lake deepened beneath the evening light until the surface burned scarlet and even the trees along the shore were wrapped in a pale red glow.

Young Klaus believed such a sight existed everywhere:

the red lake, the sun sinking beyond it, and his father and mother beside him.

Everything would remain tomorrow exactly as it was.

As a child, he had no reason to doubt it.

At home his mother prepared supper and his father fed the hearth.

The three sat around the same fire, and when Klaus grew sleepy, that broad hand touched his back.

Sometimes his mother watched them while sewing, a soft smile upon her lips.

Even after Klaus had crossed countless battlefields, attended the feasts of kings and nobles, and stood before tens of thousands of soldiers, perhaps these were the most peaceful days of his life.

Few records of his childhood survive.

Many chronicles describe the hero he became, but little remains of the boy who lived in Engosa.

Most accounts were gathered from villagers years later.

They agree on one thing:

before he was a hero or a soldier, Klaus was a boy deeply loved by his parents, living quiet days beside Lake Lacast.

That happiness ended in the winter of his eighth year.

◇

That year, war broke out on the northern border of the Principality of Ernest—

a territorial dispute with a small neighboring country.

At first, war seemed distant to Engosa.

No troops were stationed there, and the battlefield lay far away.

Travelers brought troubling rumors, but the villagers continued to till their fields and enter the forest as before.

As the war dragged on and the front demanded more soldiers, its shadow slowly reached the village.

One snowy morning, cavalry from the principality arrived.

Before the men gathered in the square, a soldier opened a sheet of parchment and read the names of those conscripted by ducal command.

Farmers, hunters, and craftsmen—

men who had sat with their families only the day before—became, one by one, men bound for war.

Klaus’s father was among them.

During the few days before departure, his parents tried to live as usual.

His father repaired the house, split wood, and finished every task he could so his family would not struggle in his absence.

His mother prepared his clothes, mended his cloak, and packed each necessity.

They scarcely spoke of the war before Klaus.

That silence was precisely what frightened him.

On the morning his father left, Klaus clung to his waist.

His father smiled helplessly, stroked his son’s head with the familiar broad hand, and said only, “I’ll be back soon.”

His mother spoke no words and adjusted his cloak to the very end.

The men began walking down the snow-covered road, and Klaus stood beside her until his father’s back vanished from sight.

Spring would bring him home.

Klaus believed it because his father had said so.

His mother, too, must have held to those words.

After he was gone, she still woke Klaus each morning, prepared meals, and lit the hearth at night.

Yet an absence unknown before remained in the house:

an empty chair at the table and an empty place beside the fire.

She put neither away.

About a month later, one conscripted villager returned, dragging one leg, his right arm in a sling and a deep wound across his cheek.

His news gave Klaus and his mother hope.

Klaus’s father was fighting bravely at the front.

He had saved many soldiers and beaten back enemy breakthroughs again and again.

That night his mother smiled peacefully for the first time in many days.

“Your father is a strong man.” The words reassured her son, but they also helped her keep believing.

Still, his father did not return.

Not when the snow melted, not when flowers returned to the shore, not when the forest deepened green and cold winds began to blow again.

Whenever wounded soldiers came back, his mother rushed from the house.

At first Klaus followed.

After enough repetitions, the distant sound of hooves alone told him what she would do.

She searched each returning soldier and every stretcher.

When his father was absent she felt relief, but relief never lasted.

She rejoiced that he was not among the dead and feared that he had not returned alive.

Waiting for him came to mean holding hope and terror at once.

Two years passed.

In the spring of Klaus’s tenth year, a soldier came to Engosa carrying his father’s effects:

bloodstained clothes, a well-used sword, several gold and silver coins in a leather pouch, and a diary.

His mother opened it with trembling fingers.

It contained neither the horrors of the battlefield nor hatred of the enemy.

“I wonder if Klaus is well.”

“When the snow falls again this year, I want the three of us to sit around the hearth.”

“My comrades are all dependable men.”

Every entry worried for his family, longed for home, or recorded some ordinary moment with his companions—

as though he had wished to hide from them how terrible the battlefield truly was.

Nothing remained to say where he died, how he lost his life, or where his body lay.

His mother received the words of mourning without breaking down and prepared Klaus’s supper that night.

Before others she smiled, worked the fields, spun thread, and tried to preserve her days as a mother.

At night, however, muffled sobs came from behind her bedroom door.

By morning she was herself again, though her body steadily grew thinner.

She moved food she could no longer eat onto Klaus’s plate, prepared his clothes before her own when winter came, and pushed a needle through torn cloth even after her fingers began to tremble.

Crushed by the grief of a wife who had lost her husband, she still refused to surrender being a mother.

Even as Klaus took on more of the household work, she worried more about him overexerting himself than about her own body.

Before spring arrived, she could no longer rise from bed.

On a snowy night, Klaus held her cold hand in a room where the hearth was dying.

She opened her eyes faintly, looked at her son, and touched his cheek with the last of her strength.

“Klaus.”

With that final word, the strength left her hand.

Klaus called to her again and again and held the hand as it grew cold, but no answer came.

Beyond the window, snow fell silently, as if to conceal the village, the forest, the crimson lake, and even the distant battlefield where his father slept.

That night, ten-year-old Klaus hated the world.

He hated the war that had taken his father;

the war that had stolen the smile from his waiting mother and finally taken her life; and above all, himself, who had been unable to do anything for either of them.

◇

After burying his mother, Klaus took up his father’s sword.

It was too heavy for his young body, and his arms trembled merely holding it in both hands, but he refused to let it go.

He swung the blade, trained his body, and opened the books on warfare left in the village each night.

Much was beyond a boy who had never seen a battlefield, but he never abandoned what he did not understand.

He read the same pages again and again.

Terrain.

Formations.

Supply.

Retreat.

Morale.

Each answer raised another question and sent him back to the books.

A sword alone could not end a war.

Knowledge alone could not protect anyone.

Klaus greedily learned everything he believed he needed.

Whenever a traveling mage stayed in Engosa, he offered work, food, and lodging in exchange for instruction.

He did not study long under any single master.

From one he learned to handle magical power, from another to interfere with the elements, and he joined the scattered knowledge together himself.

Records of this period are exceedingly scarce.

Who taught him, and what arts he acquired, remain uncertain.

Some later scholars even suggested that part of what he learned was unknown to the public even then, though no spell formula or grimoire has ever been found to prove it.

Only fragments of testimony survive.

One fact is certain:

the ten-year-old who could barely lift his father’s sword trained himself almost without rest for five years.

He ran across frozen ground in winter, struggled through mud after the thaw, and gripped the sword even when the skin of his fingers split.

When his body tired, he read.

When his mind failed, he moved his body again.

When he fell, he rose;

when too exhausted to rise, he began again the next day.

Neither honor nor advancement drove him.

To end war, he must become stronger than those who began it.

To protect another, he needed the power to repel those who would take them away.

The answer young Klaus reached was terribly simple and terribly harsh.

After five years, his father’s sword rested naturally in his hand.

◇

In the year after he turned fifteen, Klaus left Engosa and volunteered for the army of the Principality of Ernest.

The recruiting officer reportedly paid little attention to a boy who had arrived alone from a country village.

Klaus had no military record, no renowned swordmaster, and no history at a school of magic.

Once the tests began, that judgment changed completely.

His opponent in the sword examination was an adult soldier with battlefield experience, greater stature, and greater strength.

Yet there was scarcely a contest.

Klaus slipped beyond his reach without taking the force head-on and entered his guard before the man could begin his next motion.

After only a few clashes, the soldier’s weapon lay on the ground.

Changing opponents changed nothing.

Even more experienced soldiers could barely touch Klaus’s blade.

In the tactical examination, faced with a more numerous enemy, he did not fortify his defense.

He proposed deliberately yielding part of his position, drawing the enemy deep, and severing its overextended supply line to reverse the entire battle.

It was no mere trick.

His plan accounted for how the enemy would act once victory seemed certain.

The officers before the pieces on the board were said to have fallen silent for a long time.

With a sword, he overwhelmed veteran soldiers.

Given a battlefield, he turned even inferior numbers into an advantage.

The surviving army record states only one word: Accepted.

That day, the boy born in a small village beside Lake Lacast entered the army of the Principality of Ernest.

No one yet called him a hero.

He was still only one soldier among many.

But to those who know the history that followed, that day was a beginning.

The man who would reshape the history of the north itself, and leave countless legends to later ages—

The tale of Hero Klaus truly begins here.
